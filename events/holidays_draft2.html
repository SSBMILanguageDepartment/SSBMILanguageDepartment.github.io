    • Wenném Tamasím K’aw! (Happy Wintertime!) The SSBMI Language Department created this webpage to share Nisenan and Northern Sierra Miwok language for the Winter Solstice and other wintertime holidays. While Christmas and New Year's are not traditional holidays, they too provide great opportunities to celebrate your heritage by speaking your language.

    Please contact us at language@ssband.org to check on the availability of language materials or to ask any questions about language.

    Holiday language

    Click on the links below to find more Nisenan language for talking about wintertime holidays.

    Here are a few phrases we coined for wishing someone happy holidays using traditional Nisnan words and structures.

    Wenném Tamasím K’aw.

    'Happy Wintertime.'

    Wenném Beyím K’aw.

    'Happy New Year.'

    And here is a phrase we shared for wishing your friends and relations good health in the coming year.

    Honk’ayheesé wadáawesi meem hedém k’awí.

    'We hope you all will be well this year.'

    Give it a try: You can make your own holiday cards using these phrases or traditional greetings you know like séekas’in.

    Here is a traditional expression you can say when giving someone a gift; it is a command that literally means "have it".

    Heads-up: There are two ways to say this depending on whether you are talking to 1 person or a group of people. Also, this phrase differs from me (take it), which is a more general expression you can use when handing someone something.

    K’aapép.

    'I want you to have it.'

    (literal: 'Have it!', said to 1 person when giving a gift)

    K’aapewá.

    'I want you to have it.'

    (literal: 'Have it!', said to 2+ people when giving a gift)

    You can also tell someone how you got a gift using the following phrases, both of which are addressed to 1 person.

    Dootití’a ni min.

    'I bought it for you.'

    (said to 1 person)

    Siyeetí’a ni min.

    'I made it for you.'

    (said to 1 person)

    And you can thank someone using the phrase wenném, which literally means "it is good".

    Wenném.

    'Thank you.'

    (literal: 'It's good.')

    Give it a try: Say k’aapép anytime you hand someone a gift this year. Make sure to say wenném when you get a gift too!

    About the languages

    Nisenáan (Nisenan)

    The SSBMI Community has ties to the Valley and Southern Hill dialects of the Nisenan language through the Tribe’s Matriarchs, Pamela Cleanso Adams and Annie Hill Murray Paris. Pamela spoke Valley Nisenan. Based on where she was from and the language of her relatives, Annie spoke Southern Hill Nisenan. The map below shows these dialect regions and neighboring dialect and language regions.

    Map of the dialect areas of the Nisenáan language adapted from the book "California Indian languages" by Victor Golla (2011). If you would like more information or have questions, please contact us at language@ssband.org.

    The Nisenan language that we share on our learning materials is from speakers of the Valley dialect like SSBMI Matriarch Pamela Cleanso Adams and her brother Tom Cleanso, and speakers of the Southern Hill dialect like William Joseph (a.k.a. Bill Joe), Ida Hill Starkey, Charlie Hunchup, and Amanda Winn. Valley Nisenan and Southern Hill Nisenan are very similar to one another in their words and grammar, and they are more similar to one another than either one is to the other dialects of the language (i.e. Central Hill Nisenan and Northern Hill Nisenan).

    Our Language FAQ pamphlet has more information about the Nisenan language and the SSBMI Community’s language revitalization work.

    Míw’a (Northern Sierra Miwok)

    There are several distinct and mutually unintelligible Miwok languages spoken in California. Northern Sierra Miwok is the northernmost of the three Miwok languages that are spoken in the Sierra Nevada foothills. It is spoken from the Middle Fork of the Cosumnes River southward to the area between the Calaveras River and Angels Camp. It is distinct from Plains Miwok, which is spoken in the valley south of Sacramento.

    The Northern Sierra Miwok language we share comes from many speakers like Eph Cummings and others who worked with C. Hart Merriam in the early 1900s, as well as Queenie Miller, Elena McCauley, and Nicolas Villa Sr., who worked with linguist Catherine Callaghan on the Northern Sierra Miwok dictionary (1987). Our Language FAQ pamphlet has more information about the Northern Sierra Miwok language.

    About the website

    This website is maintained by SSBMI Language Department staff so that we can share Nisenan and Northern Sierra Miwok language learning resources with Tribal Members and other learners. Our team consists of: Jonathan Geary, Skye Anderson, & Richenda Ervin. Unless otherwise specified, the Nisenan and Miwok audio recordings that you will find on this website are of Jonathan, Skye, and Richenda.

    We are all learners of the Nisenan and Northern Sierra Miwok languages, and we hope that you will join us in learning Nisenan and Miwok too!

    If you have questions or want to know more about either language, you can reach out to us at Language@ssband.org.
